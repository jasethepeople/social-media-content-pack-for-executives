# Real Estate Executive Short-Form Video Scripts

## Production system

These scripts are designed for 30–45-second vertical videos for LinkedIn, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, and X. The intended voice is calm, practical, and commercially credible. The executive should speak directly to camera, use plain language, and avoid unsupported forecasts or investment promises.

| Standard | Direction |
|---|---|
| Format | 9:16 vertical video, 30–45 seconds |
| Delivery | Direct-to-camera with confident, conversational pacing |
| Visual style | Natural office, property, site, or street context; navy/teal lower thirds |
| Captions | Burned-in captions, sentence case, high contrast, 2 lines maximum |
| Hook | State the tension or counterintuitive point in the first 2 seconds |
| CTA | Invite a relevant comment, save, follow, or direct conversation |
| Compliance | Replace examples with approved facts; do not disclose confidential deal information |

## Video 1: Why cap rate alone can mislead investors

**Runtime:** 35–40 seconds.  
**Objective:** Teach investors to evaluate the quality and durability of income rather than chase a headline yield.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “A high cap rate can be a warning—not a bargain.” | Tight talking-head shot. Begin with the executive holding a printed offering summary, then lower it slightly. | **High cap rate ≠ automatic bargain** |
| 0:04–0:11 | “Before I get excited about the number, I want to know what is creating it.” | Cut to a close-up of a highlighted income statement or underwriting spreadsheet. | What is creating the yield? |
| 0:11–0:19 | “Is the income durable? Are expenses normalized? Is the rent growth assumption supported? And what happens when the debt reprices?” | Four quick B-roll cuts: occupied space, maintenance work, lease document, calculator or laptop model. | Income · Expenses · Growth · Debt |
| 0:19–0:29 | “A cap rate is a snapshot. The quality of the cash flow is the story.” | Return to the executive walking through a lobby or property exterior. Add a subtle animated line from “yield” to “cash flow quality.” | **The number is a snapshot.** |
| 0:29–0:38 | “So before comparing cap rates, compare the assumptions underneath them. That is where the real risk usually lives.” | Medium talking-head shot. End with a natural pause and direct eye contact. | Compare the assumptions. |

**Caption:** A cap rate is useful, but it is not a complete investment thesis. What assumption do you challenge first?  
**CTA:** “Comment with the metric you look at after cap rate.”

## Video 2: The three numbers I want before touring a property

**Runtime:** 35–45 seconds.  
**Objective:** Give the audience a memorable pre-tour diligence checklist.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “Before I tour a property, I want three numbers.” | Fast opening shot: executive closes a car door or enters a property. | **3 numbers before the tour** |
| 0:04–0:12 | “First: the occupancy trend—not just today’s occupancy. I want to know whether the building is improving, weakening, or hiding volatility.” | Show a simple upward/downward occupancy chart on a tablet; cut to vacant and occupied spaces. | 1. Occupancy trend |
| 0:12–0:20 | “Second: the quality of net operating income. Is it recurring, or is it being propped up by temporary items or aggressive assumptions?” | B-roll of an income statement with recurring and one-time items highlighted. | 2. NOI quality |
| 0:20–0:29 | “Third: upcoming capital needs. Roof, HVAC, elevators, tenant improvements, deferred maintenance—those costs do not disappear because they are not in the headline price.” | Slow property-detail shots: roofline, HVAC unit, elevator, maintenance corridor. | 3. Capital needs |
| 0:29–0:39 | “The numbers do not replace the tour. They make the tour more intelligent because you know what to look for.” | Executive walks through the asset, pointing toward a building system. | Numbers guide better questions. |
| 0:39–0:44 | “Use the tour to test the model—not to fall in love with the property.” | End on direct-to-camera frame. | **Test the model.** |

**Caption:** A property tour is more valuable when you arrive with questions. Which number do you want before stepping on-site?  
**CTA:** “Save this checklist for your next tour.”

## Video 3: Market headline versus market reality

**Runtime:** 35–40 seconds.  
**Objective:** Establish the executive as a nuanced interpreter of market data.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:05 | “The headline says the market is slowing. That may be true—and still incomplete.” | Begin with a newspaper, phone headline, or blurred market-news page. | **“The market is slowing.”** |
| 0:05–0:13 | “Real estate is not one market. It is an asset type, a geography, a tenant profile, and a capital structure.” | Four split-screen labels over B-roll: apartment, office or industrial building, neighborhood map, financing document. | Asset · Place · Tenant · Capital |
| 0:13–0:22 | “A luxury residential submarket can behave differently from workforce housing. Industrial can behave differently from office. A well-capitalized owner can experience the same market differently from a highly leveraged one.” | Use three or four quick property clips with clean labels. | Same market. Different exposure. |
| 0:22–0:31 | “So when you hear a broad headline, ask one more question: slowing for whom?” | Return to the executive in a street or property setting. Add the question as large text. | **Slowing for whom?** |
| 0:31–0:39 | “The goal is not to ignore the headline. It is to make it specific enough to support a decision.” | End with the executive pointing to a local map or chart. | Make the headline useful. |

**Caption:** Broad market headlines are a starting point, not an investment thesis. Add context before taking action.  
**CTA:** “Comment with the submarket you are watching.”

## Video 4: One underwriting assumption to challenge

**Runtime:** 35–45 seconds.  
**Objective:** Teach a repeatable stress-testing habit without presenting a specific investment recommendation.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “Find the assumption doing the most work in your model.” | Overhead shot of a spreadsheet. A single assumption is circled in teal. | **Which assumption does the most work?** |
| 0:04–0:12 | “It might be rent growth. It might be the exit cap rate. It might be a fast lease-up or a low operating-cost assumption.” | Zoom across model cells labeled rent growth, exit cap, lease-up, and costs. | Rent growth · Exit cap · Lease-up · Costs |
| 0:12–0:21 | “Do not just ask whether the assumption is possible. Ask how much evidence supports it and how sensitive the return is if it is wrong.” | Executive at desk, then cut to a simple sensitivity table or sliding scale. | Possible is not the same as supported. |
| 0:21–0:31 | “Then run a downside case that is uncomfortable but plausible. Slower leasing. Higher costs. A longer hold. More expensive debt.” | B-roll of a timeline extending, construction invoice, interest-rate document, and vacant suite. | Stress the plausible downside. |
| 0:31–0:40 | “The strongest model is not the one with the most attractive base case. It is the one that survives a serious challenge.” | Return to executive, close the laptop, and face camera. | **A strong model survives scrutiny.** |

**Caption:** Underwriting is not about defending the deal. It is about learning what the deal needs in order to work.  
**CTA:** “Save this for your next investment committee review.”

## Video 5: What makes a location durable

**Runtime:** 35–40 seconds.  
**Objective:** Move the audience beyond the cliché of “location, location, location.”

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “A great location is not just a pin on a map.” | Drone or street-level establishing shot; avoid relying on a famous landmark alone. | **A location is more than a pin.** |
| 0:04–0:13 | “I look at access, jobs, infrastructure, supply, affordability, and the quality of daily life.” | Six quick B-roll clips: transit, employment district, road or bridge, construction crane, local storefronts, people walking. | Access · Jobs · Infrastructure |
| 0:13–0:22 | “But I also ask what the location is becoming. Is connectivity improving? Is demand diversifying? Is new supply solving a problem—or creating pressure?” | Show a map animation, construction activity, and a neighborhood transition. | What is the location becoming? |
| 0:22–0:32 | “The best locations usually have more than one reason to remain relevant. That is what creates durability.” | Executive walks outdoors through the neighborhood. | **Durability comes from multiple demand drivers.** |
| 0:32–0:39 | “Study the place today, but underwrite the place it could become.” | End with a wide establishing shot and a subtle branded lower third. | Study today. Underwrite tomorrow. |

**Caption:** Location is not static. The right question is what is changing around the asset and whether those changes strengthen durable demand.  
**CTA:** “What demand driver makes your market durable?”

## Video 6: The operator’s advantage

**Runtime:** 35–45 seconds.  
**Objective:** Show that operating quality is a strategic differentiator, not an administrative detail.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “Two similar buildings can produce very different outcomes.” | Match-cut between two similar exteriors or two lobby entrances. | **Same asset type. Different outcome.** |
| 0:04–0:12 | “The difference is often not the building. It is the operating system around the building.” | Show a property manager reviewing a dashboard, speaking with a vendor, and walking a common area. | The operating system matters. |
| 0:12–0:21 | “How quickly are issues resolved? How well are renewals managed? Are maintenance problems prevented or simply repaired? Do vendors have clear accountability?” | Four B-roll shots keyed to each question. | Response · Retention · Prevention · Accountability |
| 0:21–0:31 | “These details influence tenant experience, operating cost, reputation, and ultimately the durability of cash flow.” | Show tenant interaction, maintenance log, positive review, and a simple cash-flow line. | **Operations become performance.** |
| 0:31–0:40 | “Operations are not overhead. They are strategy in motion.” | Strong direct-to-camera close, preferably inside the property. | **Operations are strategy in motion.** |

**Caption:** Operational excellence is difficult to see in an acquisition brochure, but it becomes visible in retention, response time, cost control, and reputation.  
**CTA:** “Follow for more practical real estate operating insight.”

## Video 7: A deal lesson from difficult negotiations

**Runtime:** 35–40 seconds.  
**Objective:** Offer a memorable negotiation principle that demonstrates executive maturity.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “The best negotiators do not fight every position.” | Close-up of two hands reviewing a term sheet; no confidential text visible. | **Do not fight every position.** |
| 0:04–0:12 | “They separate three things: the person, the position, and the underlying constraint.” | Three words appear one at a time beside the executive. | Person · Position · Constraint |
| 0:12–0:20 | “A seller may insist on price because they need a certain net outcome. A tenant may resist a term because they need flexibility. The stated position is not always the real problem.” | Use neutral B-roll of a meeting room, whiteboard, or two parties reviewing documents. | The stated position may not be the real problem. |
| 0:20–0:30 | “Once you understand the constraint, you may be able to solve it with timing, structure, certainty, or a different trade—not just a bigger number.” | Show a whiteboard with the words timing, structure, certainty, trade. | Solve the constraint. |
| 0:30–0:38 | “Separate the person, the position, and the constraint. That is where better solutions begin.” | Direct-to-camera conclusion. | **Better solutions begin with better questions.** |

**Caption:** Negotiation improves when you stop treating every position as fixed and start identifying the constraint underneath it.  
**CTA:** “What negotiation principle has served you best?”

## Video 8: How to make a market update useful

**Runtime:** 35–45 seconds.  
**Objective:** Provide a repeatable framework for turning data into executive commentary.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “Do not just report what changed. Explain what it means.” | Begin with a market report or chart on screen, then cut to the executive. | **Data is not insight yet.** |
| 0:04–0:11 | “A useful market update has four parts.” | Four-part graphic animates in. | **4-part market update** |
| 0:11–0:18 | “One: what changed? Two: why did it change?” | Executive counts one and two; overlay simple chart movement and arrow. | 1. What changed?  2. Why? |
| 0:18–0:27 | “Three: who is affected? Buyers, sellers, tenants, lenders, owners, or developers may not experience the change the same way.” | Cut to labeled audience groups and relevant property footage. | 3. Who is affected? |
| 0:27–0:35 | “Four: what decision should this influence? Buy, hold, improve, refinance, lease, or wait.” | Show the six decision words appearing around the executive. | 4. What decision follows? |
| 0:35–0:43 | “That is the difference between commentary and insight: connecting the number to a decision.” | Final direct-to-camera shot with branded lower third. | **Connect data to decisions.** |

**Caption:** A market update becomes valuable when it explains the change, the cause, the affected audience, and the decision that follows.  
**CTA:** “Save this four-part framework for your next market update.”

## Video 9: The hidden cost of indecision

**Runtime:** 35–45 seconds.  
**Objective:** Reframe waiting as a decision with measurable cost and information value.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “Waiting has a price.” | Close-up of a clock, calendar, or construction timeline. | **Waiting has a price.** |
| 0:04–0:12 | “Waiting can be smart when it helps you learn something specific. But if you cannot say what you are waiting to learn, it may just be indecision.” | Executive beside a whiteboard with “What are we waiting to learn?” | What are we waiting to learn? |
| 0:12–0:21 | “While you wait, carrying costs continue. Competitors move. Options change. Financing, pricing, and availability may not stay still.” | B-roll of calendar pages, interest-rate document, competing construction site, and a property listing changing. | Carrying costs · Competition · Options |
| 0:21–0:31 | “So define the question, set a time or evidence threshold, and decide what action follows each possible answer.” | Show a simple three-step checklist appearing beside executive. | 1. Define  2. Set a threshold  3. Decide |
| 0:31–0:41 | “If the answer is unclear, waiting is not a strategy. It is a pause with carrying costs.” | Return to direct-to-camera, measured delivery. | **Wait strategically—or move.** |

**Caption:** Waiting only creates value when it creates information. Make the cost of delay visible before calling it patience.  
**CTA:** “What information would change your next decision?”

## Video 10: The leadership principle I use in every cycle

**Runtime:** 35–45 seconds.  
**Objective:** End the series with a values-based leadership message that can represent the executive’s broader brand.

| Time | Spoken script | Visual and B-roll direction | On-screen text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:04 | “The leadership principle I use in every market cycle is simple: clarity compounds.” | Direct-to-camera opening in the executive’s office or on-site. | **Clarity compounds.** |
| 0:04–0:12 | “When the thesis is clear, the team knows what matters. When ownership is clear, problems surface earlier.” | B-roll of a team reviewing a plan and one person marking an owner beside a task. | Clear thesis. Clear ownership. |
| 0:12–0:21 | “When communication is clear, partners can make decisions with better information. And when escalation is clear, bad news arrives while there is still time to act.” | Team meeting, phone call, and an escalation path drawn on a whiteboard. | Clear communication. Clear escalation. |
| 0:21–0:31 | “Clarity does not mean pretending the future is certain. It means making the uncertainty visible and deciding what matters most.” | Executive walking through a property with a subtle overlay of scenario branches. | Clarity is not certainty. |
| 0:31–0:41 | “In uncertain markets, clarity is a competitive advantage. It helps good teams move with discipline instead of reacting to noise.” | Final hero shot, direct eye contact, then brand mark or handle. | **Move with discipline.** |

**Caption:** Strong leadership does not require perfect certainty. It requires clear priorities, visible assumptions, and disciplined action.  
**CTA:** “Follow for practical real estate leadership and market insight.”

## Repurposing guidance

Each finished video can produce a caption-only LinkedIn post, a short X thread, a quote graphic, and a newsletter paragraph. Use the hook as the social headline, the middle section as the educational body, and the closing principle as the quote card. For compliance and credibility, replace generic examples with approved local data, anonymized transaction lessons, or clearly labeled illustrations before publication.

## Final production checklist

Before recording, confirm the executive’s exact wording, the market or asset context, and whether any example requires client or legal approval. Record one clean direct-to-camera take and one alternate take with a shorter hook. Capture B-roll in vertical format with no confidential documents visible. Add captions, a branded lower third, and a single CTA. Review the final cut for unsupported statistics, promises of returns, disclosure of private information, and statements that could be interpreted as individualized investment advice.
