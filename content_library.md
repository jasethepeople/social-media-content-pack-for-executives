## 60 LinkedIn posts

### Day 1: The market is not “good” or “bad.” It is specific.

The useful question is not whether real estate is up or down. It is: **up or down for whom, in which submarket, and under what capital structure?**

A seller, a first-time buyer, an apartment owner, and a developer can experience the same market very differently.

The best operators replace headlines with three questions:

1. What changed?
2. Who is exposed?
3. What decision does this change require?

That is how market commentary becomes decision support—not noise.

### Day 2: Cap rate is a symptom, not a strategy.

A cap rate can look attractive and still hide a weak investment.

Before celebrating the yield, ask:

• Is the income durable?
• Are expenses normalized?
• Is the growth assumption defensible?
• What happens when debt reprices?
• Who is the next buyer?

The number matters. The story behind the number matters more.

Which assumption would you challenge first?

### Day 3: The strongest deal memo begins with a “no.”

A disciplined investment process does not begin by proving why a property should be bought.

It begins by trying to disqualify it.

What would make the thesis fail? Which assumption is doing the most work? What evidence would change our mind?

If the deal survives honest skepticism, the conviction is earned.

Optimism is useful in real estate. Unchallenged optimism is expensive.

Share this with someone evaluating a property.

### Day 4: Why “location, location, location” is incomplete.

Location is not a single variable. It is a bundle of access, demand, supply, regulation, infrastructure, talent, and perception.

A great location today can become an average one if the tenant base changes or new supply arrives. An overlooked location can improve when connectivity and jobs follow.

The better question is: **what is this location becoming?**

People often celebrate the closing and forget the months of work that made it possible.

The real value is usually created earlier: in the questions asked, the risks surfaced, the expectations aligned, and the points of friction removed.

### Day 6: The hidden cost of waiting.

Waiting can be wise. It can also be an expensive form of indecision.

The question is not “Should we wait?” It is “What are we waiting to learn, and how will that information change the decision?”

If the answer is unclear, waiting is not a strategy. It is a pause with carrying costs.

Rent growth is an assumption. Operations, retention, service, and capital planning are the work that makes the assumption possible.

### Day 8: A good renovation solves a problem.

The most successful capital improvements are not the flashiest. They solve a resident, tenant, maintenance, or leasing problem.

Before approving a project, ask:

1. What friction does it remove?
2. Who values the improvement?
3. How will we measure the result?

Aesthetic upgrades are easy to notice. Operational improvements are easier to compound.
